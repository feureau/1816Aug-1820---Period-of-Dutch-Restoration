## SKETCHES OF SOCIETY.

### LETTERS FROM PARIS.—NO. XVI.

*Paris, April 20th, 1821.*

I was present, a few days ago, at the annual public sitting of the society for the encouragement of national industry. This society has been established for one and twenty years, and it seems to have been formed on the plan of that which exists in London, with the difference, that it is more powerfully supported by government: for in all parts of the continent, but particularly in France, governments superintend institutions of that kind, even private ones.

The sitting was opened with the report of the Secretary General, Baron de Gerando, which commenced by stating that the stagnation of trade, which, since the restoration of trade, since the restoration of trade, had been observable in all parts of the world, also exercised its influence in France, though perhaps not so great a degree as in countries less fertile, or less rich in natural productions. The reporter remarked a truly singular change which has taken place in trade in general, at least throughout the French departments; namely—that in proportion as education has been diffused among the industrious classes, their views have become more extended than formerly. Instead of being content to receive the raw materials from the hands of intermediate agents, that is to say, from traders and brokers, they now endeavour to procure them directly from the places of production, thus placing themselves in immediate relation with the agricultural and producing classes. M. de Gerando observed, this change had proved highly advantageous to trade; the manufacturer obtains his materials cheaper and more genuine, and can better command the articles he is in want of; while the producer, in his turn, knows perfectly what he has to supply, through the manufacturer. Through the direct relations which have proved equally advantageous to both classes, the importance of warehousing, discounting, and banking transactions, has considerably diminished. There are now scarcely any magazines or depots of merchandise in France; and last year the district of the bank of Paris fell almost one half. In England, however, different opinions are probably entertained on this subject; and what M. de Gerando regards as an advantage or perfection, might there be considered a defect in the present state of things: for it appears to me, that the establishment of intermediate agents or merchants, denotes the perfection of trade, and that their absence marks its infancy. But perhaps I may be wrong, therefore I will not take upon me to speak decidedly.

The reporter of the society likewise called attention to the incalculable mischief which nations mutually occasion to each other by the numberless restrictions imposed on foreign importations. He hoped that the period was not very remote, when all would be convinced that trade can only prosper in proportion as people are permitted freely to export and import; the concessions should be reciprocal among nations; that those who produce much, must also consume much; and that it is unblushingly unjust to prevent the miserable inhabitant of all countries, and especially of his own, to prolong his human misery can prompt any agreement to persevere in their present system, of which all Europe feels the ill effects.
